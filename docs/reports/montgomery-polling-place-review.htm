Montgomery County, Ohio · November 3, 2026 election

33 precincts got new polling places. Did the moves favor a party?

We found no sign of it. The Montgomery County Board of Elections moved 33 of the county's 381 precincts to new polling places for the November 3 election. With so many people asking hard questions about how elections are run, it's fair to ask whether those moves landed harder on one party's voters. We checked, using the state's public voter records.

What changed

The Board's notice lists 20 changes covering 33 precincts in eight cities and townships. Fourteen old buildings are out and 17 new ones are in. Most precincts moved together as a group to one new building.

WHIO TV 7 reported the list on October 28. Board director Jeff Rezabek told the station the changes were announced more than a month ago, and the Board is mailing notices to the voters affected.

Two listed changes aren't new for voters. Clayton 3-A, 3-B and 3-C still vote in the same building, which has a new name. And Dayton 8-A, 8-B and 8-C have likely voted at Horace Mann Elementary since May 2025, when news reports said their old site, Southview Children and Family Center, had closed. We count both anyway, because the Board listed them.

Two label mix-ups in the published lists

Vandalia J, not I. WHIO's list first read “Vandalia C, F, & I.” The story and the Board's notice now say J, and the map agrees: J borders C and F. We use J.

Clayton is missing its ward. “Clayton A, B, & C” fits three different wards. The changed precincts are 3-A, 3-B and 3-C.

Did the moves favor one party? We see no pattern.

The precincts that moved are no more Republican or Democratic than the precincts that stayed put in their own towns.

When the Board stopped using a polling location, every precinct that voted there moved together. So we compared each of those 14 groups with the rest of its own town. If the moves were aimed at one party, most of the bars below would point the same way. They split 7 and 7, and the long bars point both ways.

Put together, the moved groups lean about 1.4 points toward Republicans, a gap chance alone produces about 62 times out of 100. The data shows no favoritism toward either party.

How we measure lean, and what the colors mean

In an Ohio primary, each voter asks for one party's ballot, and the state keeps a record of which one. For each voter, we look at the Republican and Democratic primary ballots they've taken, with recent primaries counting more. Then we average over the precinct's active voters who have taken a party ballot at least once (53% of voters).

  • Strong R: more than 75% of these ballots are Republican.
  • Lean R: 57.5% to 75% Republican.
  • Competitive: neither party above 57.5%.
  • Lean D: 57.5% to 75% Democratic.
  • Strong D: more than 75% Democratic.

Part of that comes from where the changes happened: they're bunched in a few towns, and 8 of Miamisburg's 14 precincts changed. It doesn't favor either party: Lean R and Lean D precincts both changed more often than average. And it hinges on one precinct. Dayton 8-A, one of the precincts that have likely voted at Horace Mann since May 2025, sits just past the line between Competitive and Lean D.

The numbers behind the check

We made three comparisons. For each one we asked: if the moved precincts had been picked at random, how often would a difference this big or bigger show up? A tilt toward either party counts the same.

None of these is rare. Our best guess for the last one is 1.4 points toward Republicans, and the true gap is likely somewhere between about 7 points toward Republicans and about 4 points toward Democrats. That range includes zero. Leaving out Clayton and Dayton 8-A, 8-B and 8-C, which may not have really moved this year, gives almost the same answer: 1.5 points toward Republicans, a gap chance gives about 62 times in 100.

What this check can't see. With only 14 old polling places, it would catch a tilt of about 7 or 8 points toward either party, but a smaller one could slip by. Lean reflects which party's primary ballot voters choose, not how they vote in November. And no voter data can explain why a particular location was chosen.

How the shuffling works. For the first comparison, a computer picked 33 precincts at random 20,000 times and counted how often the random picks differed as much as the real ones. For the second, it did the same but kept the number of moved precincts in each town fixed. For the last, we treated each of the 14 old buildings like a coin: each flip decides whether that building's gap points toward Democrats or Republicans, and its size stays the same. We checked all 16,384 ways the 14 coins could land.

For the lean-band table: across the whole county, a pattern that uneven would come up by chance about 2 times in 100. Within towns, it's about 3 or 4 times in 100. The within-town number is the fairer one, because the moves are bunched in a few towns. The unevenness runs toward both parties, not one, and it rests on Dayton 8-A, less than half a point past the Lean D line. Count it as Competitive and the within-town number goes back to about 14 times in 100.

How far the polling places moved. We measured the driving distance from each old building to its new one. Within towns, polling places for Republican-leaning precincts moved a little farther, about 0.4 miles or 1 minute more, but that's the kind of gap chance often produces, and this check could miss a gap smaller than about 1 mile or 2 minutes. Across the whole county, without the within-town comparison, they moved about three-quarters of a mile farther, a gap chance gives about 4 times in 100. We ran several versions of this check, so one result that low isn't surprising, and it doesn't show up in drive time. We can't tell whether it has to do with party or just with where the buildings are. These are distances between buildings, not each voter's own trip.

Reading the bars: the three Clayton precincts that left Mt. Calvary Lutheran lean about 10 points more Democratic than the rest of Clayton. The two Dayton precincts that left ATU Local 1385 lean about 21 points more Republican than the rest of Dayton.

How to think about a polling-place change

Moving polling places is routine. Buildings close, owners change their plans, and sites can fail accessibility checks. But every move asks voters to do something new, and that's where trust and turnout come in. Three questions are worth asking about any change. They're also the questions people raised in an r/dayton discussion of these changes.

1 Who got moved?

If one party's precincts got moved much more than the other's, that would be a red flag. This is the question we checked, and we didn't find that here. Ohio builds in a check, too: every county board of elections has two Republicans and two Democrats (R.C. 3501.06).

2 How much harder is it to vote now?

Research finds that moving a polling place can lower turnout, mostly because some voters don't learn about the move and go to the old site. A well-known study of a countywide shake-up in Los Angeles in 2003 saw this even though the moves weren't aimed at either party. Montgomery's change is far smaller, so its effect has to be measured here, not borrowed. The moves were short. For 30 of the 33 precincts, the typical new polling place is about a mile, or a 4-minute drive, from the old one, and none is more than 3 miles away. In a county where most people get around by car, those are easy trips. (Clayton's three precincts didn't move.) Within each town, we found no clear difference in how far polling places moved for Republican-leaning and Democratic-leaning precincts.

Know something about these locations that the data can't show, like parking, stairs or bus access? Email motorbikematt@precincts.info.

3 Was it done in the open, and on time?

Ohio law says a board can't change a polling place in the 25 days before an election, except in an emergency (R.C. 3501.18). That rule covers making the change, not mailing the notice. The Board's director says the changes were announced more than a month ago, and we take the Board at its word.

The real problem is communication. Notices reaching voters about a week before Election Day leave little time to plan, and label mix-ups in the published lists, like the two we caught, make it harder to trust what you read.

Trust is easier to build when the work can be checked. The Board's list, the state voter file and every calculation on this page are public.

What we'll check next: turnout

A few weeks after November 3, election records will show who voted, and whether they voted on Election Day, early in person or by mail. We'll compare the moved precincts with similar precincts in the same towns that didn't move, using each precinct's 2022 turnout as the starting point. The clearest warning sign would be fewer Election Day voters in the moved precincts without a matching rise in early or mail voting. A drop of 2 or 3 points should show up. Whether one party's voters were hit harder is a tougher question: with only 33 precincts, only a large gap would be visible.

Technical appendix: statistical methods

Hypothesis and statistic. H0: which precincts had their polling place changed is independent of precinct partisan lean, conditional on municipality. The test statistic is the difference in mean precinct lean, changed minus unchanged, with lean on [−1, 1]. All tests are two-sided.

Lean. For voter i, Li = Σk wksik / Σk wk over the voter's partisan primary ballots k, with s = +1 (Democratic) or −1 (Republican) and wk = exp(−0.15 tk), where tk is the ballot's age in years at the snapshot (half-life about 4.6 years). Precinct lean is the mean of Li over ACTIVE voters with at least one partisan primary ballot (53% of 365,310 registrants; 381 precincts). (1 + L)/2 and (1 − L)/2 are exactly the average voter's recency-weighted Democratic and Republican shares, not the pooled ballot shares; a lean difference Δ equals Δ/2 in share points.

Negative values are toward Republicans. Permutation p-values are (count + 1)/(N + 1). The 95% interval for the building-level mean is −0.136 to +0.077 (percentile bootstrap, 10,000 resamples of the 14 events), about 6.8 points toward Republicans to 3.9 toward Democrats.

Power. With 14 building-level clusters, the minimum detectable effect at 80% power is about 0.15 lean (about 7.7 points). Smaller effects cannot be ruled out.

Band analysis. A χ2 statistic on the 5 × 2 table (lean band × changed), referred to a permutation null rather than the asymptotic distribution: naive null (33 of 381 drawn uniformly) p ≈ 0.016; municipality-matched null (changed count fixed within municipality) p ≈ 0.035. Changes are spatially clustered (8 of Miamisburg's 14 precincts), so the matched null is the right reference. Both nulls reject at 0.05. The excess is symmetric: Lean R (16 of 133 changed) and Lean D (8 of 43) are over-represented against Competitive (5 of 119). It is sensitive to the cutoffs: Dayton 8-A (lean +0.157) sits 0.007 above the +0.15 boundary, and scoring it Competitive gives matched p ≈ 0.14.

Robustness. Including CONFIRMATION-status voters leaves the results unchanged. Secondary metrics (share of voters with a partisan primary ballot, primary participation rate, mean age, precinct size) show no difference under the same tests.

Limitations. Lean measures primary-ballot choice, not general-election vote share, and is undefined for the 47% of voters without a partisan primary ballot. One voter-file snapshot (October 24, 2026). The design tests selection only; burden is measured as driving distance and time from old to new building (Google Maps routes; Clayton = 0), tested against lean at the building level with the same design (200,000 permutations): within-municipality p = 0.91 for miles and 0.34 for minutes (slope); unadjusted p = 0.04 for miles and 0.10 for minutes, among about eight specifications. Straight-line change from precinct centroids is not reported, because most changes are smaller than the centroid's error. Fourteen clusters limit power, and the analysis is silent on intent. Map: the boundary file is labeled 2022 (all 381 codes match the current voter file); building locations are geocoded; the voter center is the voter-weighted mean of precinct centroids.

Precinct
The smallest voting area. Everyone in a precinct votes at the same polling place.
Polling place
The building where a precinct's voters vote in person on Election Day.
Party primary ballot

Active voter
A registered voter in good standing.
Confirmation status
A voter the county has sent a notice asking them to confirm their address. They can still vote.
